# Supervising a looped depth backbone, and the failure of adaptive repetition

**Question.** A depth-recurrent policy — a small transformer core repeated K times, with readouts after chosen repetitions — raises two supervision questions that a plain transformer does not have. First, what should each intermediate readout predict: every readout on the strongest teacher target, or a progression of increasingly strong targets matched to increasing depth? Second, can test-time computation be adapted per position — running more loops on harder positions — to beat simply fixing the loop count? Several recipe choices were open alongside: the form of the value loss, an auxiliary board-prediction loss, and whether readouts need private per-exit adapters.

**Method.** Host model: a 4-layer transformer core repeated 3 times (12 executed layers, ~31M parameters, hidden 768) on 991,808 Stockfish-annotated (depth 16) human-game positions, two epochs, batch 512, one seed, 8,192 held-out positions scored against the depth-16 teacher's best move, all key comparisons paired with bootstrap intervals. Supervision attaches at three readout points (after 4, 8, and 12 executed layers); in the progressive schedule they predict the depth-1, depth-9, and depth-16 teacher targets respectively. Rounds proceeded result-dependently: capacity (plain 12-layer vs three recurrence layouts at equal effective depth and FLOPs); schedule × readout form; a six-cell adapter ablation (default/zero init × residual/replace/gated × two learning rates); value loss (cross-entropy vs cross-entropy + scalar regression) × auxiliary loss (none vs one-step future board occupancy); elasticity measurement (running the trained trunk 1–6 loops and scoring every exit); variable-loop training (sampling the loop count per batch); and scale rechecks along seed, width (+74% parameters), data (×2 positions, ×2 updates), loop placement (five architectures at fixed effective depth 16 and identical FLOPs), and sharing-vs-width at locked parameter count.

**Evidence.**

- Repetition up to 3× is nearly free at equal effective depth: the 4×3 host with 31M parameters tied an 87M plain 12-layer model (2.891 vs 2.898 nats), and a 6×2 layout with half the parameters slightly beat it. Extreme sharing (2×6) pays a visible tax.
- The progressive schedule wins decisively and stably: 2.713 vs 2.853 (all exits on the strongest target) vs 2.891 (final readout only). The predicted mechanism behind uniform supervision's failure — gradient conflict between exits — was falsified directly: gradients between exits stayed positively aligned throughout (cosine 0.40–0.64 progressive, 0.90–0.99 uniform). Uniform supervision hurts not by conflict but by erasing the coarse-to-fine division of labor between exits; dense supervision on early exits is itself a strong signal (a 4-layer early exit under uniform targets already beats the 12-layer final-only model).
- No per-exit adapter variant ever beat the plain shared head: gated tied within noise, lower learning rates and zero init were mildly worse, replace was much worse (2.967). The adapters were actively used (gates measurably nonzero) yet bought nothing — exit differentiation is carried by the trunk's representations, and private readout capacity has no function to serve at this scale. The adapter form is retired.
- The one-step board-occupancy auxiliary (predict, per square, what the board looks like after the teacher's move) helps in both value-loss conditions (−0.014/−0.018 nats), with the largest gain at the shallowest exit — falsifying the sub-prediction that deeper exits should benefit more. Adding a scalar regression term to the value loss hurts in both conditions (+0.030–0.034): the scalar fit improves while move prediction pays the price. Settled supervision: move cross-entropy + value cross-entropy + the board auxiliary.
- Elasticity of the trained trunk: quality peaks at the 8-layer readout (2.674/0.274) and degrades monotonically beyond (12 layers: 2.699; 24 layers: 2.886). Value quality peaks deeper, at the 12-layer readout. An oracle that picks the best exit per position at equal compute would gain +0.21 nats / +5 top-1 points — but every cheap deployable rule (convergence tests, confidence thresholds) is dominated by simply fixing K=2, because whether the answer will change is predictable while the size of the gain is not (and, counterintuitively, low-confidence positions are not the ones that gain).
- Training with per-batch variable loop counts halves the degradation past the peak but does not move the peak, and costs +0.04–0.05 nats uniformly across the usable range (5× the pre-registered tolerance). Adaptive repetition is rejected at this scale; the deployment form is a fixed loop count with split readout (move from the 8-layer exit, value from the 12-layer exit).
- The peak at 8 of 12 executed layers is robust: it reproduces across seeds (seed noise band ~0.011 nats), at +74% width (peak unmoved, overall quality −0.06 to −0.08), and with doubled data and updates (peak unmoved, quality −0.25 to −0.29 at every exit, peak-to-next gap shrunk by a third). The "deepest exit is undertrained" explanation is rejected as the main mechanism. The same peak-then-degrade shape appears in every architecture tried, including one with zero weight sharing — so it is not caused by recurrence either.
- Sharing itself is neutral currency, not a virtue or a vice: at equal FLOPs, more parameters win (a zero-sharing 115M stack is best; an extreme 10M shared layout is far worse); at equal parameters, more compute wins (a shared-and-widened layout at 2× and 4× the FLOPs gains −0.040/−0.050, with the second doubling already saturating). Quality consumes parameters and compute alike; weight sharing only trades one for the other.
- Internal health was monitored throughout (residual norms, effective rank, attention entropy, dead units): no instability or collapse anywhere; the progressive recipe carries an elevated final-layer residual norm (growing with sharing intensity and training volume, up to 40) without observable harm, and heavily shared wide models show declining attention entropy alongside the largest train–validation gap — an over-specialization signature worth rechecking at hero scale.

**Conclusion.** The settled supervision recipe for a looped depth backbone is: progressive teacher-depth targets across readouts, one shared readout head, move + value cross-entropy, and a one-step board-occupancy auxiliary. Rejected with direct evidence: per-exit adapters, scalar-augmented value loss, uniform-depth targets, and adaptive per-position repetition in both its cheap-rule and learned-base forms. A consistent finding across all rounds is a depth-degradation peak at 8 of 12 executed layers: intrinsic to this model and scale, architecture-independent, unmoved by width, data, or training duration (only its height shrinks), and unexplained — the surviving candidates are noise in the deepest teacher targets and over-specialization of the final loop. Whether recurrence belongs in the deployment architecture at all, given that plain multi-exit depth matches it at equal FLOPs, is deferred to hero-scale architecture selection alongside the pending sequence and combined arms. All conclusions are single-seed, two-epoch, ~30–115M-parameter results; final appeal belongs to hero scale.
